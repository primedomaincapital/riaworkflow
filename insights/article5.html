The $100 Million Floor: Fidelity’s Custody Minimum and the Great RIA Sorting

RIA custody & consolidation · 6 min read

The letter arrived at a small advisory firm sometime this autumn, and it was polite, short, and final: Fidelity was raising its minimum for RIAs to $100 million, and firms below the line had until June 30, 2027 to reach it or begin leaving. A few hundred firms are affected, holding less than one percent of Fidelity's clearing and custody assets. The numbers are small. The message is not. The custody business has decided that the smallest RIAs are no longer worth the operational cost of serving — and the firms on the wrong side of that line now have eighteen months to industrialize their operations, merge, or find a new home.

The sorting is already underway

Fidelity's floor is only the latest push in a consolidation wave that has been building for years. Echelon Partners counted 142 announced RIA transactions in the first quarter of 2026, moving $1.67 trillion in transacted assets — more than double the first quarter of 2025. Fidelity Institutional recorded 276 completed RIA transactions in 2025, against 233 in 2024, moving $796.4 billion of purchased assets, up 19 percent. There were 102 unique buyers, a record, and 88 percent of deals were backed by private equity. Cerulli Associates reports that 54 percent of all RIA firms are seeking an acquisition, and projects more than 26,000 advisor retirements over the next decade — the largest single source of deals in the market. Firms with more than $5 billion now manage 54 percent of all RIA assets, up from 34 percent in 2018, while making up just 2 percent of firms.

The deal tape from the past few weeks reads like a roll call. Wealth Enhancement agreed to acquire RWA Wealth Partners, with about $22.46 billion in client assets. Concurrent Investment Advisors acquired Spire Investment Partners, with roughly $5.4 billion in assets and more than 30 advisor teams, taking Concurrent to $28.6 billion in assets under management from $16.8 billion at the start of 2026. Beacon Pointe bought three RIAs totaling about $1.35 billion, its ninth deal of 2026. CAPTRUST added the Hoge-Barrett Group, a 13-person team serving more than $3 billion. Meanwhile the wirehouses lost 1,449 experienced advisors in the first half of 2026 and recruited only 932 — a net loss of 517 across Merrill, Morgan Stanley, UBS, and Wells Fargo.

Operations are what is actually being bought

Here is the part the deal announcements skip: almost none of these transactions are about investment performance. They are about operations. A firm with $400 million in assets and fifteen disconnected systems — a CRM that does not talk to the portfolio system, a planning tool that exports to spreadsheets, a billing process that runs on one person's memory — cannot absorb another firm's clients without breaking. The acquirers know it. That is why the buyers are consolidators with centralized technology, compliance, and back-office teams, and why the sellers are firms whose founders are retiring and whose operations were never built to scale.

The technology gap is the tell. One advisor who joined Savant described spending 80 percent of his time getting ready for meetings and patching together fifteen to twenty different tools — and then, after the merger, using Jump AI for meeting notes through the firm's stack and calling it a huge time saver. The point is not the notetaking tool. It is that a large firm can evaluate, approve, and deploy a tool in weeks while an independent firm spends months deciding and then must run compliance itself. Operations, not asset management, is the scarce resource. The firms being acquired are selling their client relationships; the firms doing the acquiring are buying the capacity to serve them.

The counterargument: independence is not dead

Consolidation is not the only direction of travel. XY Planning Network reports recruiting has spiked as advisors leave rollups, with solo shops launching at nearly two a day. Betterment is waiving a fee through 2028 to win the sub-$100 million firms Fidelity is pushing out, and Schwab's custody head notes his firm already serves 11,000 micro-practices. The same technology that makes scale valuable — cheap AI notetaking, automated onboarding, outsourced compliance — also makes independence cheaper than it has ever been. A two-person firm with the right stack can now serve clients as smoothly as a firm ten times its size, at least on the things clients actually see.

Both stories are true, and they point to the same conclusion. The middle is disappearing. Firms are either building industrial-grade operations — in-house or through a platform — or they are joining someone who already has. The firms in trouble are the ones in between: too big to be a lifestyle practice, too small to afford the stack, and too proud to admit the choice is now forced.

What this means for RIA operators

Fidelity's June 2027 deadline is the forcing function, but the logic applies to every firm under a billion dollars. Audit the workflow honestly: how many systems touch a new client before the first meeting, how many hours of advisor time go to preparation rather than advice, and what breaks if your best operations person leaves. If the answer is uncomfortable, there are three moves — build the stack, join a platform, or sell — and all three are cheaper and less disruptive now than they will be in eighteen months, when a few hundred firms are making the same decision at the same time. The custody floor is not really about custody. It is the market telling small RIAs that workflow is no longer a back-office concern. It is the business.
