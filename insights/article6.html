What Does RIA Workflow Automation Cost to Implement?

Workflow automation costs · 8 min read

The partners of a $600 million RIA outside Columbus met on a Tuesday in February to approve a workflow platform. The quote on the table was $48,000 for the first year: licences, implementation, training. One partner called it expensive. Another called it cheap. Both were guessing. Nobody in the room could say what the firm already spent on the work the software was supposed to replace — the retyped client data, the chase for signatures, the Friday afternoons lost to billing. That is the normal state of this decision. Firms buy workflow automation the way people buy a car after only checking the sticker price, and the sticker is the smallest part of the bill.

The bill nobody budgets for

Start with the software itself, because that is the only number vendors volunteer. Kitces Research puts typical RIA technology spending at 4 to 6 percent of revenue, and tracks more than 300 software products built for advisors — a market so crowded that integration, not features, is now the top driver of advisor satisfaction. For a solo practice, the honest first-year range is $15,000 to $30,000 in total operating costs, according to LaunchAdvisor's 2026 startup budget guide; a full-featured practice runs $35,000 to $60,000. Those figures cover compliance, software, insurance, and basic marketing — not a single platform, but the whole cost of opening the doors.

The component prices explain why the totals spread so wide. Redtail, the most widely used advisor CRM, charges $99 a month for up to 15 users. RightCapital, now the second most-used comprehensive planning tool with about a quarter of advisors naming it their primary planning software, runs $150 to $250 a month. eMoney, the long-standing incumbent, starts above $500 a month. Portfolio management is the heavyweight: Orion, the most widely used platform for mid-size firms, runs roughly $2,500 to $5,000 a month for a practice with 30 to 100 households, about $70 per household per month. Envestnet's Tamarac suite typically runs $10,000 to $30,000 a year depending on assets. A firm that buys the category leaders in every box can spend $60,000 a year on licences before a single workflow is automated. A firm that buys carefully can spend a third of that and notice little difference in daily work.

Integration: the line item vendors skip

The second cost is the one that never appears on a quote: making the tools talk to each other. The 2026 T3/Inside Information Software Survey found 91.08 percent of responding advisors use one of the 17 surveyed CRMs — the CRM is settled infrastructure, not a choice. The problem is everything plugged into it. A planning tool that exports to a spreadsheet, a portfolio system that will not sync household data, a document vault that needs manual filing: each gap is a person doing robot work. Kitces' 2025 AdvisorTech survey found advisors rate integration as the most important driver of satisfaction with their stack, ahead of the quality of any single tool. That is a polite way of saying firms are paying for software that does not work together and then paying staff to bridge the gaps by hand.

Integration bills arrive in three forms. There is the vendor's implementation fee, often 20 to 50 percent of the first year's licence cost, charged for configuration, data migration, and workflow design. There is the consultant, typically $10,000 to $25,000 for a mid-size firm to map processes and build the automations properly. And there is the hidden one: the weeks when the operations lead is building workflows instead of serving clients. Firms that skip the first two usually pay the third for years. The cheapest implementation is rarely the one with the lowest invoice. It is the one that finishes.

“The software is the cheapest part of automation. The expensive part is the firm you have to become to use it.”

Staff time is the real invoice

The largest cost is also the easiest to ignore because it never appears on a vendor invoice: staff time. Kitces' research on how planners actually spend their weeks found the average planner works about 43 hours a week, spends only 19 percent of it meeting clients, and devotes 46 percent to delegable middle-office work — plan and meeting preparation, client servicing, and administration. The average financial plan takes 15 hours to produce and deliver, and 34 hours of total advisor and team time across three meetings. That is the cost base automation attacks. Every hour a workflow returns is an hour that was already being paid for.

Put a number on it and the payback argument sharpens. Suppose a four-advisor firm recovers five hours a week per advisor — a modest claim for automated meeting prep, data gathering, and follow-up. At a loaded cost of $150 an hour, that is $3,000 a week, or about $150,000 a year in recovered capacity. Against a $50,000 first-year platform cost, the payback is four months. The arithmetic is illustrative, not a promise: it assumes the recovered hours go to revenue work rather than longer lunches, and it values an advisor's hour at a rate many small firms would dispute. But the direction is robust. Schwab's 2024 RIA Benchmarking Study, covering 1,304 firms and $2 trillion in assets, found top-performing firms — the heaviest adopters of digital tools and standardized workflows — spend about 25 percent less time per client on operations and about 10 percent more time per client on service. The prize is not a lower software bill. It is a different allocation of the same payroll.

The new AI layer adds a fresh cost line firms should price with open eyes. Anthropic's Claude for Financial Advisors, launched in September 2026, requires a 20-seat Enterprise minimum at $20 per seat per month — $4,800 a year before a single query — and InvestmentNews reported usage running roughly $70 to $120 per user per month on top. A ten-advisor firm could face $8,400 to $14,400 a year in usage alone, by one commentator's estimate. A six-person firm buying 20 seats pays for 14 it does not have. AI meeting notes and drafting are genuinely useful. They are not free, and the seat minimums are designed for firms far larger than the ones most tempted by them.

The counterargument: the cheap stack wins

There is a serious case for spending less, and it starts with an uncomfortable observation: most firms automate badly because they buy before they fix the process. A workflow that encodes a broken onboarding sequence just produces bad onboarding faster. The firms that get the most from the least tend to share a discipline — they standardize the process on paper first, buy the smallest stack that fits, and add tools only when a named person can say which hours the tool returns. Redtail at $99 a month plus a custodian's free tools will carry a solo practice a long way. Altruist, the digital custodian, charges advisors nothing for custody, account opening, billing, and reporting. The gap between a $12,000-a-year stack and a $60,000-a-year stack is often five figures of features nobody opens.

There is also the risk that automation becomes its own bureaucracy. Every new tool needs an owner, a renewal decision, a security review, and a place in the compliance manual. Kitces tracks more than 300 advisor products; the average firm uses up to 12 technology solutions across 20 business functions. Each addition is a dependency. Firms that buy a platform for every problem end up with a stack that needs a full-time administrator — which is how a tool bought to save headcount creates one.

What this means for RIA operators

Price the decision the way an acquirer would: total first-year cost, including implementation and the staff hours the rollout consumes, against the hours the system returns, valued at what those hours earn when redeployed. A solo advisor should expect $15,000 to $30,000 all-in for year one and should refuse any platform whose implementation fee exceeds its first-year licence. A $500 million firm should budget $50,000 to $100,000 for software plus implementation, and should measure success in advisor hours per client, not in features switched on. And every firm should ask the vendor the question that ends most sales conversations early: which specific hours does this return, for whom, in the first ninety days — and what happens to the price when the firm grows past the tier it was sold? The firms that answer those questions before signing are the ones whose automation pays for itself. The rest buy a subscription and keep doing the work by hand.
