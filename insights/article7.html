How Long Does It Take to Onboard a New Client at a Modern RIA?

Client onboarding timelines · 7 min read

The client said yes on a Thursday. She signed the advisory agreement on Friday, sent the funding paperwork the following Monday, and then heard nothing for eleven days. No call, no portal login, no confirmation that her money had moved. On day twelve she phoned her old broker to ask whether the transfer had even started. It had not. The forms were sitting in a queue, waiting on a signature page nobody had flagged as missing. She did not fire the advisor — the money eventually arrived, six weeks after she said yes — but she told two friends the firm felt disorganized, and both of them stayed with their brokers. Onboarding is where a new client relationship is made or quietly lost, and the clock that matters is not the one on the custodian's wall. It is the one in the client's head.

The honest timeline

For a straightforward taxable account moving to a modern RIA, the realistic span from signed agreement to funded, invested account is about three weeks. Clearfolio, which builds onboarding software for RIAs, puts it bluntly: the 21-day window is mostly waiting, not working. The work itself — data entry, form generation, review — takes hours. The waiting takes weeks, and it takes weeks in specific places that firms can name if they bother to look.

Day one to day three is the paperwork phase: the advisory agreement, the investment policy statement, risk tolerance, beneficiary designations, and the custodian's new-account forms. Done digitally, with pre-filled data and e-signature, this takes a day. Done on paper, or with forms emailed back and forth as PDFs, it takes a week — and every round trip adds a day. The single most common delay is the not-in-good-order rejection: a mismatched account title, a missing signature, a date in the wrong format. Each NIGO rejection costs days per client, and it costs them at the worst possible moment, when the client is newest and least forgiving.

Then comes the transfer. For assets moving between brokerage firms, FINRA Rule 11870 sets the clock: the delivering firm has one business day to validate the transfer instruction or raise an exception, and three business days after validation to deliver the assets. A clean ACATS transfer completes in about six business days. Schwab charges $50 for a full transfer out and nothing for a partial one; Fidelity charges nothing either way. But the rule only starts when the instruction is submitted correctly, and the exceptions are where timelines die: a title mismatch, a non-transferable asset, an annuity that cannot move through ACATS at all and can take 30 days or more. Retirement accounts add their own paperwork — successor custodian acceptance, rollover forms — that can add one to three business days before the clock even starts.

Where the days actually go

The pattern is consistent enough to state plainly. The custodian's processing is rarely the bottleneck. The bottlenecks are the handoffs: the client who does not return a form, the advisor who does not notice it is missing, the operations associate who discovers on day nine that the account title on the transfer form does not match the title at the delivering firm. FastTrackR's analysis of wirehouse-to-RIA transitions makes the same point from the other side: advisors who hit 45-day transition timelines, down from 90-plus five years ago, do it by running operational work in parallel with the SEC's 45-to-90-day registration review, not after it. The work is not hard. The sequencing is everything.

That is why the digital onboarding tools matter, and why their claims deserve a hard look. OnBord, the onboarding platform, launched a Schwab Advisor Center integration on November 3, 2026 that lets advisors open new Schwab accounts in four steps, entirely digitally, and — in the company's words — take account opening from weeks to minutes while cutting NIGO errors. The claim is vendor marketing, and minutes-to-open does not mean minutes-to-funded: the ACATS clock, the client's own delay in sending money, and the delivering firm's exceptions all still apply. But the direction is real. The firms that onboard in days rather than weeks have removed the same three frictions every time: pre-filled forms, e-signature with validation at entry, and a single owner tracking each account from signature to funding.

Where automation only moves the bottleneck

Here is the part the software demos skip. Automating the forms does not automate the client. A retiree who needs a week to find a paper statement, a trustee who must get a co-trustee's signature, a business owner whose CPA has the cost-basis records — none of that yields to a better portal. Neither does the compliance review: the firm's own anti-money-laundering checks, the review of outside assets, the documentation of suitability. A firm that automates account opening and leaves compliance review as a silent eleven-day gap has not sped up onboarding. It has sped up the first mile and left the client waiting in the same place, now with higher expectations.

Schwab's 2024 RIA Benchmarking Study, covering 1,304 firms and $2 trillion in assets, found top-performing firms spend about 25 percent less time per client on operations than the median firm — and about 10 percent more time on service. Read that as an onboarding instruction: the hours saved on forms should be spent on the client, not banked. The firms with the fastest onboarding are not the ones with the flashiest portals. They are the ones that call the client on day two, confirm the transfer was submitted on day four, and phone again the day the money lands. Communication is the cheapest automation there is, and the one most firms skip.

The counterargument: fast is not always better

Speed has a cost, and it is worth naming. A firm that opens and funds accounts in 48 hours has also compressed the time it has to catch mistakes — a wrong beneficiary, a misunderstood risk tolerance, an account funded with money the client needed for a tax bill. The compliance failures that end advisory careers rarely come from firms that moved too slowly. They come from firms that moved too fast and documented too little. There is also the client's own pace: some people need a week to sit with a decision that moves their life savings, and a firm that pressures them through a same-day funnel can feel less like service and more like a sales floor. The right question is not how fast can onboarding go. It is how fast should it go for this client, with this complexity, without cutting the checks that protect both sides.

What this means for RIA operators

Measure onboarding the way the client experiences it: days from signed agreement to funded account, counted separately for each stage — paperwork, custodian submission, transfer, funding — so the bottleneck has a name and an owner. A straightforward taxable account should land inside three weeks; anything past thirty days deserves an explanation, not a shrug. Automate the parts that are genuinely mechanical — pre-filled forms, e-signature, validation at entry, status tracking — and spend the hours you save on the parts that are not: the day-two call, the transfer confirmation, the funding-day welcome. And audit the NIGO rate the way you would audit a billing error, because it is one: every rejected form is a client reconsidering the decision to hire you. Onboarding is the first product a new client buys. Most firms treat it as paperwork. The firms that treat it as the product keep the client.
