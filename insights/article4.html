The Consolidation Wave: What RIA M&A Means for Operations

tech-enabled RIA operations · 4 min read

In January 2024, a $3 billion RIA in the Midwest announced its acquisition by a national aggregator. The press release used all the right words: “cultural alignment,” “shared vision,” “enhanced client experience.” The deal closed in March. By September, three of the acquired firm’s top advisors had left, taking $400 million in client assets with them. The acquiring firm’s CEO called it “normal attrition.” The advisors who left called it something else: operational chaos.

They described a technology migration that broke client reporting for weeks. A CRM consolidation that lost years of contact notes. Compliance procedures that contradicted each other. Back-office staff who quit rather than learn a third new system in two years. The merger made strategic sense on paper. Operationally, it was a slow-motion disaster.

This is the hidden story of the RIA consolidation wave — the largest restructuring of independent financial advice in history, now entering its most challenging phase. The deals are getting bigger, the integrations are getting harder, and the operational infrastructure that determines success or failure is being tested like never before.

The scale of consolidation

The numbers tell a story of accelerating concentration. DeVoe & Company, the leading tracker of RIA M&A, has documented record deal volumes for most of the past decade. 2024 saw another banner year, with total transactions exceeding 250 and aggregate acquired AUM in the hundreds of billions. The buyers fall into three categories: national aggregators (Focus Financial Partners, Hightower Advisors, Mercer Advisors), private-equity-backed platforms (Wealth Partners Capital Group’s portfolio companies, Reverence Capital Partners’ vehicles), and the emerging class of mega-RIAs doing serial acquisitions to achieve scale.

The strategic logic is compelling. Scale brings negotiating power with custodians and technology vendors. It enables investment in capabilities — alternative investment platforms, tax planning, estate services — that small firms can’t afford alone. It provides succession solutions for aging founders, a demographic imperative given that the average RIA owner is in their late 50s. And in a business where organic growth has slowed as markets matured, acquisition is the fastest path to asset growth.

Private equity’s role can’t be overstated. Firms like Hellman & Friedman (backing Edelman Financial Engines), Bain Capital (backing Carson Group), and Reverence Capital have poured billions into RIA platforms, betting that the independent advice channel will continue taking share from wirehouses and that scaled platforms will command premium valuations. The typical PE playbook — acquire, integrate, professionalize, scale, exit — is now the industry’s dominant operating model.

Where integrations break

Ask M&A veterans where deals go wrong, and they rarely mention valuation or deal structure. They mention operations. Specifically, they mention five recurring failure points.

Technology stacks. Every RIA has a tech stack — CRM, portfolio management, financial planning, document management, compliance surveillance. No two are identical. Merging them means choosing winners and losers, migrating data, retraining staff, and managing the inevitable period where nothing works quite right. The most common disaster: the acquirer’s “superior” platform turns out to be worse for the acquired firm’s specific workflows, but the decision is irreversible because the old system’s contracts were terminated.

Client experience. Clients chose their advisor for a reason — often the personal relationship and the firm’s specific service model. Post-merger “harmonization” of service models frequently means the acquired firm’s clients get a worse experience: less access to their advisor, more call centers, standardized portfolios replacing customized ones. The J.D. Power wealth management satisfaction studies consistently show that acquired clients report lower satisfaction than organic ones, and the gap persists for years.

Advisor retention. The advisors are the business. When they leave, the assets follow. Yet acquirers routinely underestimate how disruptive integration is for producing advisors. New compliance procedures, unfamiliar technology, loss of autonomy, cultural mismatch — each is a reason to consider the competing offer from the RIA down the street that’s been calling for months. Industry data suggests that deals losing more than 10% of acquired advisors in the first two years rarely achieve their financial projections.

Back-office consolidation. The business case for most acquisitions includes cost synergies — eliminating redundant operations staff, consolidating offices, centralizing functions. But the back office is also where institutional knowledge lives. The operations manager who knows every client’s quirks, the compliance officer who understands the firm’s specific regulatory history, the client service associate who remembers birthdays — these aren’t redundancies. They’re the infrastructure of trust. Cutting them to hit synergy targets often costs more in client attrition than it saves in salaries.

Culture. The most cited and least measurable factor. RIAs are founder-led businesses with distinct personalities. Aggregators promise autonomy but deliver standardization. The tension is structural: the acquirer needs consistency for scale, the acquired firm needs distinctiveness for client retention. Managing this tension requires more than town halls and branded swag. It requires genuine operational flexibility — the willingness to let different offices do things differently, which is exactly what scale is supposed to eliminate.

The operations advantage

The firms getting M&A right share a common trait: they treat operational integration as the deal’s primary challenge, not an afterthought. This means several specific practices.

First, they conduct operational due diligence with the same rigor as financial due diligence. Before closing, they map both firms’ technology stacks, document every workflow, interview back-office staff, and identify integration risks explicitly. The best acquirers walk away from deals where the operational fit is poor, regardless of the financial attractiveness.

Second, they invest in integration infrastructure before they need it. This means dedicated integration teams (not advisors doing it part-time), standardized playbooks refined across multiple deals, and technology platforms designed for multi-entity operation from the start. The serial acquirers who do 10+ deals per year — Mercer Advisors, Wealth Enhancement Group, Mariner Wealth Advisors — have turned integration into a core competency.

Third, they measure what matters. Not just AUM retention (which can mask underlying problems if markets are rising), but advisor satisfaction, client satisfaction scores, employee turnover, and time-to-full-integration. The firms that track these metrics obsessively are the ones that improve deal over deal.

Technology is becoming the differentiator. The next generation of RIA platforms is being built API-first, with the assumption that acquisitions will continue and that technology stacks must be composable rather than monolithic. Firms like Altruist (custody), Advyzon (all-in-one platform), and a new wave of AI-powered operations tools are designing specifically for the multi-entity, post-merger RIA. The acquirers who adopt modular, integration-friendly technology will have a structural advantage over those running legacy monoliths.

The counterargument: maybe consolidation is good

It’s worth steelmanning the case for consolidation, because it’s stronger than critics admit. Small RIAs often lack the resources for robust cybersecurity, sophisticated investment platforms, or comprehensive compliance programs. Scale enables investment in capabilities that benefit clients: better technology, deeper planning expertise, access to alternative investments, more resilient business continuity.

There’s also the succession argument. Thousands of RIA founders are approaching retirement with no internal succession plan. Without acquirers, many of these firms would simply wind down, leaving clients to find new advisors in a disrupted transition. Acquisition, even imperfect acquisition, is often better than dissolution.

And the competitive dynamic cuts both ways. Wirehouses and national firms aren’t standing still. For independent RIAs to compete — on technology, on talent, on client experience — they need scale. The alternative to consolidation isn’t a golden age of boutiques. It’s slow decline as larger competitors out-invest and out-execute.

The question isn’t whether consolidation will continue. It will. The question is whether the industry can solve the operational challenge fast enough to make the deals work for clients, advisors, and shareholders simultaneously. Right now, that’s an open question.

What this means for RIA operators

For RIA leaders — whether buying, selling, or staying independent — the consolidation wave has clear operational implications.

If you’re acquiring: invest in integration capability before you need it. The firms that do deals well treat it as a discipline, not an event. Build the playbook, hire the team, choose technology for composability. And be honest about cultural fit — the best financial deal with the wrong cultural fit is the worst deal.

If you’re selling: conduct your own operational due diligence on the acquirer. Talk to advisors at firms they acquired two years ago, not the references they provide. Ask specifically about technology migration, autonomy, and advisor retention. The press release promises are free. The operational reality is what you’ll live with.

If you’re staying independent: the bar is rising, but independence remains viable — and for many firms, preferable. The key is operational excellence as a competitive strategy. The independents thriving in the consolidation era aren’t competing on scale. They’re competing on service quality, niche expertise, and the client experience that large platforms struggle to replicate. But this requires deliberate investment in operations, not just hoping that being small is enough.

The RIA industry is being remade. The firms that understand operations as strategy — not overhead — will write the next chapter. The ones that treat it as an afterthought will be footnotes in someone else’s acquisition press release.
